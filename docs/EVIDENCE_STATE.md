# The Economy of Bits — state of the evidence

3 September 2026. Nine notebooks. The empirical work is complete.

---

## 1. What the paper can claim

**Concentration rose, and the first paper's numbers survive repair.** On a panel with
2016 coverage lifted from 391 firms to 403, the Hill tail index falls 1.4669 → 1.1413
(*p* = 0.0062, published 1.491 → 1.141 at *p* = 0.006), the Gini rises 0.5879 → 0.7186, the
top 1% share 11.0% → 30.0%. Every published claim holds.

**It is not explained by the economics of bits.** Bit intensity built from accounting
statements predicts membership of the top 25% (*p* < 0.0001), 10% (*p* = 0.003) and 5%
(*p* = 0.024), and is flat at the top 1% (*p* = 0.96). Worse for the theory: in a
Gabaix–Ibragimov rank regression, high-bit-intensity firms have a **thinner** tail — +0.299
ranked within sector, *p* = 0.0002. The sign is wrong, and it is robust to
sector-neutralisation.

**Growth remains Gibrat-like.** Growth on size +0.007, *p* = 0.12, with no bit-intensity or
platform interaction. The tail is not generated by increasing returns to size.

**The firms that run compute became capital-intensive, and it is those firms specifically.**
Hyperscalers against other tail firms, capex extra trend +0.0139, *p* = 0.0003, firm fixed
effects. Against everyone else, +0.0102 (*p* = 0.0043). But **tail against everyone else is
+0.0038, *p* = 0.27**. "Large firms became capital-intensive" is false.

**Dated: a cloud-era reallocation that AI accelerated.** Hyperscaler capex intensity was
already rising significantly before the paper's window — 2008–2015 slope +0.0041,
*p* = 0.0022 — then 3.5× faster from 2016 (+0.0146, *p* < 0.0001). Design layer capital
intensity has fallen for eighteen years, 0.107 → 0.022. The fabricators' burden is recent:
flat to 2015, +0.0062 (*p* < 0.0001) after.

**Calibrated, not asserted.** Hyperscalers travelled from −3% of the distance between a
software and a utility cost structure in 2008, to 7% (2015), 28% (2020) and **64% (2025)**.
Two-thirds of the way. They are not utilities and the paper should not say they are.

**Capital did not buy value share.** Lagged capex on share growth: −0.115 (*p* = 0.067) with
firm and year fixed effects; −0.435 (*p* = 0.19) in the cross-section. Both negative.

**The value went to the layer that owns no plant** — with an honest caveat. Decomposition of
the 2016–2025 change in index share: operates compute +9.78, designs compute +10.27, other
digital +4.51, compute equipment +0.65, fabricates compute −0.20, not digital −25.03. But
**Nvidia is 69% of the design layer's gain**; without it the layer gains +3.18, and the
trend survives (+0.331 pp/yr, *p* < 0.0001) while the magnitude collapses. The operating
layer is a genuine category: largest member 34%, +6.51 points without Alphabet.

**Out of sample, thirty years: half confirmed.** The fabricator decline is structural —
1996–2015 slope −0.186 pp/yr, *p* = 0.0015, with Intel going from 5.19% of the top hundred
to 0.38%. The designer rise is **not** confirmed: −0.037 (*p* = 0.079) before 2016. The
paper dates it and declines to call it structural.

**Intel is the argument in one firm.** Capital intensity 0.138 (2008) → 0.475 (2023),
outspending every hyperscaler, while its share of index value went 0.908% → 0.165% (2024).
Spending peaked as share bottomed. Execution and process failures are the larger story and
the paper says so; what the data shows is that capital commitment and value capture moved in
opposite directions, which the bit-economics account does not predict.

**The tail churns.** Ten firms passed through a five-slot top 1%; only Apple and Microsoft
are there all ten years. Shorrocks mobility 0.50.

## 2. Objections already closed

**Lease accounting (ASC 842).** Four ways. Capex is a cash-flow measure leases cannot touch;
capex and asset lightness correlate at −0.774; the decline survives on plain-tag firm-years
(−0.0073, *p* < 0.0001); and with the actual right-of-use figure subtracted, hyperscaler
asset lightness in 2025 is **0.5579 against 0.5841 as reported** — removing lease assets
makes them look *heavier*, so leases were masking the decline, not creating it. The fall
also begins in 2010–2012, years before the standard took effect.

**Survivorship and renaming.** The panel lost firms when their ticker changed; 19 curated
renames with EDGAR evidence restore them, and the headline numbers move by at most 0.004.

**Composition.** Every trend reported on both an unbalanced and a balanced ten-year panel.

**Post-hoc classification.** The layer taxonomy rests on one rule — who owns the fab —
applied identically, with the boundary cases (Apple, Tesla) reported under three treatments.
The operating result is +9.79 under all three; the design result ranges +10.28 / +13.54 /
+15.98, and the paper quotes the range.

## 3. Two defects to state in the limitations

**The exclusion rule punishes fast growth.** Notebook 09 drops firm-years where revenue
falls below half the firm's own median, to catch mis-tagged revenue (Charter at $1.1bn
against real revenue near $55bn; the tower REITs likewise). It also drops **Amazon
2008–2013**, whose revenue is legitimately below half its 2008–2025 median because it grew
fifteenfold. Comparing to the previous year instead of the median cuts exclusions from 361
to 151 and, with Amazon's early years restored, the pre-2016 hyperscaler slope goes from
+0.0041 to **+0.0108** — the conclusion is unchanged and the trend is stronger. The patch is
one line; either version supports the claim.

**Berkshire reports no PP&E** under the standard tag in any year and is excluded from the
capital measures. One firm, named.

## 4. What the paper argues

Firm-level concentration in the S&P 500 rose steeply between 2016 and 2025, and the standard
account — non-rival goods, zero marginal cost, demand-side scale — does not explain it.
Firms whose output is most bit-like sit in the upper middle of the distribution and, within
their own sectors, have *thinner* tails. Growth stays proportionate.

What happened instead is a reallocation *within* the digital economy, along the compute
stack. The firms that operate compute took on capital at a rate that has carried them
two-thirds of the way to a utility cost structure. The firms that fabricate it have been
losing value share for thirty years. And the firms that design it — owning no plant — gained
as much index value as the operators on a quarter of the capital, though one company supplies
most of that gain.

The economics *Machine, Platform, Crowd* describes did not disappear. They moved to the layer
whose product is a design rather than a machine. Which layer captures the surplus from a
general purpose technology is not a property of the technology, and that is where the paper
meets Acemoglu.
