# The Economy of Bits — research plan

Companion paper to *Are Firms More Unequal Than People?* (JRFM, under review).
Version 1.0 · 2 September 2026

---

## 1. What the first paper left open

The JRFM paper established the fact: between 2016 and 2025 the Pareto tail index of S&P 500
market capitalisation fell from 1.491 to 1.141, the top 1% share rose from 11.3% to 30.0%,
and **the entire aggregate movement sat in firms classified as digital by GICS sector**.
Non-digital firms ended the decade where they began (1.582 → 1.596, *p* = 0.63).

That result is a correlation with a label. "Information Technology" and "Communication
Services" are administrative categories, not economic mechanisms. Six firms carried the
result and all six happen to be in those sectors, but nothing in the analysis says *why*.
The sector split cannot distinguish between three quite different stories:

1. digital firms are asset-light and therefore scale cheaply;
2. digital firms sell goods with near-zero marginal cost of reproduction;
3. digital firms sit on two-sided networks where each new user makes the product better.

*Machine, Platform, Crowd* names all three and separates them. This paper measures them.

## 2. The theoretical move

Brynjolfsson and McAfee's chapter 6 argues that information goods are **free, perfect and
instant**, and that the third property is what makes the first two economically live: a
drive full of identical copies is worthless until a network can deliver them. Chapters 7
and 8 add the strategic consequences — paying complements, two-sided pricing,
cross-elasticity, and the finding that the largest network keeps winning because switching
costs and multi-sided benefits compound.

The important distinction for a firm-size distribution is this. Gibrat's law, which
underlies most of the heavy-tail literature, describes **supply-side** scale: growth
proportionate to size, independently drawn. Network effects are **demand-side** scale: a
user's benefit rises with the number of other users, so growth is *not* independent of
size. Two different generating processes, two different predicted tails. The first paper's
apparatus can tell them apart; it just was not pointed at them.

Acemoglu supplies the reading of whatever we find. *Power and Progress* argues that a
technology's direction — automating versus augmenting — is chosen, not given, and that who
chooses depends on who holds power. *Why Nations Fail* draws the inclusive/extractive
distinction. Chapter 8 of *Machine, Platform, Crowd* describes platform owners who first
"remove long-standing barriers that have kept people from transacting" and then "influence
the flow of transactions so that more benefits go to the platform owner" — the same
transition, inside one firm's rulebook. A falling tail index is a fact; whether it records
value *created* or value *captured* is the question those books pose.

## 3. Hypotheses

Each is stated so that it can fail.

| # | Claim | Prediction | Falsified if |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H1** *Free* | Near-zero marginal cost, not sector, drives tail position | Tail index falls monotonically across bit-intensity quartiles | Bit intensity adds nothing over the GICS split |
| **H2** *Instant* | Two-sided networks carry extra weight beyond asset-lightness | Platform firms have a lower tail index than pipeline firms **at the same bit intensity** | The platform coefficient vanishes once bit intensity is controlled |
| **H3** *Mechanism* | The tail is generated by demand-side, not proportionate, growth | Growth rates correlate with size; growth-rate variance declines with size more slowly than Gibrat implies | Growth is independent of size — Gibrat survives |
| **H4** *Persistence* | Concentration may be transitional, as it was under electrification | Top-decile membership churns; Shorrocks mobility is well above zero | The same firms hold the tail for ten straight years |
| **H5** *Validation* | The firm measure tracks an independent industry measure | Industry bit intensity from BEA IPP capital correlates with the firm-level index | The two measures are unrelated |

H4 is the one that could most embarrass the first paper, which is why it is in. Shaw
Livermore found 40% of the trusts formed between 1888 and 1905 had failed by the early
1930s; Richard Caves found the average market share of firms dominant in 1905 and still
alive in 1929 fell from 69% to 45%. Concentration under a general purpose technology has
historically been a phase, not an end state. Ten years cannot settle that, and the paper
will say so.

## 4. Measurement

### 4.1 Bit intensity (firm level, annual)

A composite of four standardised components, each a direct reading of one of the book's
claims. Equal weights, fixed in advance; PCA as robustness.

| Component | Formula | Reads |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue | marginal cost of reproduction ≈ 0 |
| R&D intensity | R&D / Revenue | value in bits, not plant |
| Asset lightness | 1 − (Net PP&E / Total assets) | no physical distribution constraint |
| Revenue per employee | Revenue / employees | scaling without headcount |

Components are z-scored **within year** so the index measures position in the
cross-section, not a common time trend that would confound it with the market.

### 4.2 Two-sidedness (firm level, static)

A binary hand-coding against three criteria, all of which must hold:

1. the firm intermediates between two identifiable groups it prices separately;
2. participants on one side benefit as the other side grows;
3. the firm sets the rules of interaction rather than owning the inventory.

**The coding is fixed before any tail index is estimated** and is committed to the
repository with a one-line justification per firm, so it cannot be tuned to the result.
Ambiguous cases are coded as pipeline (the conservative choice against H2).

### 4.3 Tail and concentration

Unchanged from the first paper, deliberately: Hill estimator with *k* = 10% of *n* floored
at 10, Clauset–Shalizi–Newman fits with Vuong comparisons against lognormal and
exponential, Gini, top shares, HHI. Reusing the validated apparatus is what makes the two
papers comparable. Both α conventions are reported on the survival basis.

### 4.4 Persistence

Ten-by-ten transition matrices over market-cap deciles; retention rate of the top 1% and
top decile; Shorrocks mobility index. This has no counterpart in the first paper and is the
clearest new empirical content.

**Why EDGAR and not yfinance for fundamentals.** `yfinance` returns four years of annual
financials per ticker. The panel needs ten. EDGAR's XBRL `frames` endpoint returns every
filer reporting a given concept in a given period in one call — roughly 80 calls for the
whole panel instead of 550 — with `companyfacts` as the per-firm fallback. This is the main
engineering task of notebook 01.

## 7. What would make this paper not worth writing

Stated now, so it is not rationalised away later:

- If EDGAR coverage of COGS and R&D falls below ~70% of firm-years, the bit-intensity
  index is too thin and the design has to change.
- If bit intensity turns out to be collinear with the GICS digital split (|r| > 0.9), the
  paper has renamed a variable rather than explained one.
- If the platform indicator has fewer than about 25 firms, H2 cannot be tested at the tail
  sizes the Hill estimator needs.

Notebook 01 measures all three.

## 8. Threats already known

- **Survivorship, again.** Fundamentals are only filed by firms that still exist. Firms
  that left the index mid-decade will have truncated financials. The market-cap panel
  handles this through reconstructed membership; the fundamentals panel must report
  coverage by year so the same problem is visible rather than silent.
- **Ten annual observations** is thin for HAC inference. Report OLS *p*-values alongside.
- **The platform coding is a judgement**, and the honest defence is that it is fixed in
  advance, documented per firm, and conservative in ambiguous cases.
- **Bit intensity is measured from accounting statements**, which record what firms report,
  not what economists mean. Gross margin is a proxy for marginal cost, not marginal cost.
